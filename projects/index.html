Stippenkaart 1941

I like old maps. When digitizing them, I find myself drawn in by the subtle defects, like rips in the paper, typos, and squiggles in hand-drawn lines. The stippenkaart was different. It was created by an Amsterdam city employee to show where Jewish citizens were residing. The occupying authorities used it as a tool in the mass deportation and murder of approximately 60,000 people. When I came across it several years ago, I was struck by the symbology of using dots to represent people, and an idea to turn it into a memorial has been in the back of my mind ever since. With recent improvements in agentic A.I. tools, I was able to actualize the concept of using dots to symbolize human souls. I finished this project in about a two-week sprint, and at times, I was genuinely brought to tears.

A New YouTube Channel Experiment

For this project, Hoog and I tried an experiment. Using an advanced AI-assisted workflow, we found failed housing projects that were documented by local government bodies. With these videos, we shined a light on the NIMBY tactics and byzantine rules that prevent housing from being built. I was involved in the curation, scriptwriting, and geographic information processing. The last of these included querying a self-hosted PostGIS database, performing Python-based spatial analysis to determine alley lots, and cleaning data in QGIS.

Why L.A. Became a Dystopia

I researched and wrote this script for Hoog about Los Angeles. The piece is focused on how streetcar suburbs were built by private companies before the city was transformed into a car-centric hellscape. It tells the story about a series of unfortunate policy decisions, shifting consumer habits, and a conspiracy that went all the way to the top.

Mapping U.S. Housing Affordability

I used R to compile data from the U.S. Census Bureau, creating a nationwide dataset on housing and population characteristics. From this, I calculated the ratio of median house price to median household income, a standard way to measure housing affordability. I mapped the results across ~32,000 communities, grouping them into clear affordability categories. To make the map easy to read, I used a dark background for contrast, a high-visibility color scale, and kept labels and legends simple. The result is a national map that shows housing affordability patterns clearly and at a glance.

Redesigning a Historical Map

I digitized and redesigned an archival map of Paris that showed plans for highways through the city center. The original map used similar colors and symbols for different features, which blurred distinctions and created clutter. I corrected this by designing a new symbology that establishes hierarchy between map features, improving clarity and usability. The redesigned version employs an accessible, colorblind-friendly palette based on the work of Paul Tol.
